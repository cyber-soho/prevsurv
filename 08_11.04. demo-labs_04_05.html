0.2 The "teach a newcomer" quality bar

This is the single most-asked question every year: how detailed should the report be?

The answer: detailed enough that a friend of yours who has never opened Wireshark, never seen a Windows Event Viewer, and has no idea what a SHA-256 hash is, could pick up your report and learn the workflow purely by reading it. That standard governs every page you write.

In practice this means:

  • Every click, command, and keystroke is shown verbatim. "Open Wireshark" is not enough; "Open Wireshark by double-clicking the desktop icon — Figure 3" is.
  • Every screenshot is annotated. A red rectangle, an arrow, and a one-line caption explaining what the reader is supposed to see.
  • Every acronym is expanded the first time it appears: NIC (Network Interface Card), SOC (Security Operations Center), MFT (Master File Table).
  • Every finding has a why. "The flow to 23.55.12.9 is suspicious because (a) the destination is in a non-Canadian ASN, (b) the burst happened at 02:07 EST when the office is closed, and (c) no proxy log shows a user clicking a link to that host."
  • Every command's output is included (copy-paste in a fenced code block) — not just a screenshot, because text is searchable and screenshots are not.

If a stranger could replicate your work by reading your report, you have met the bar. If they could not, the report is incomplete.

2.2 Confirm the sensor is in promiscuous mode

A NIC (Network Interface Card) not in promiscuous mode discards every frame whose destination MAC (Media Access Control) address is not its own — meaning your sensor will silently miss 70 %+ of the network it is supposed to defend. This is the classic blind spot.

Run the promiscuous-mode check script from Lecture 1:

cd ~/cyber-soho-code/
chmod +x L1_check_promisc.sh
./L1_check_promisc.sh ens33

Expected output, abbreviated:

iface=ens33
flags = 0x1303  →  IFF_PROMISC bit set
ip -d link show ens33  →  'promiscuity 1'
Both sources agree.  Sensor is OK.

If the script says promisc OFF, set it manually for the session:

sudo ip link set ens33 promisc on

…then re-run the check.

6. Step 4 — Identify five kinds of traffic (30 minutes)

This is where capture filters end and display filters begin. A capture filter throws frames away before they are written; a display filter is non-destructive — it merely hides what does not match. You can change display filters without losing data.

For each of the five rows below:

  1. Type the display filter into the green bar at the top of the main Wireshark window.
  2. Press Enter.
  3. Observe the filtered packets.
  4. Take a screenshot.
  5. Write the one-line interpretation in your report.
What Display filter What you should see
DNS (Domain Name System) queries dns Query / response pairs for cbc.ca and the other lookups you did.
HTTPS handshakes tls.handshake.type == 1 Client Hello frames containing the SNI (Server Name Indication) — readable.
ICMP (Internet Control Message Protocol) echoes icmp The four echo requests + four echo replies to and from 9.9.9.9.
ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) chatter arp "Who-has 192.168…" / "Is-at aa:bb…" pairs on the local segment.
Anything you did NOT expect filter you invent something — see below.

6.1 The "surprise" row — why we ask for it

Real captures are full of things you did not initiate. The lab VM may chat with the time-sync server, your operating system may probe LLMNR (Link-Local Multicast Name Resolution), Windows may announce itself over NetBIOS, an mDNS service may advertise the printer, a DHCP renewal may happen mid-capture. Pick one unexpected protocol you see, write one paragraph about it, and answer:

  • What is it?
  • Should I be concerned?
  • If yes, what would I do next? If no, what would have to change for it to become a concern?

8. Alternative open-source tools you may explore

If you finish early or want extra credit, run one of the same workflows with one of the tools below and add an appendix to your Lab 4 chapter comparing the two:

  • Zeek — instead of raw packets you get semantic logs (conn.log, dns.log, ssl.log, http.log). Often the right first look at a large capture.
  • tshark — Wireshark's command-line companion. Same dissectors, scriptable.
  • Brim / Zui — opens multi-gigabyte pcaps Wireshark cannot, with a SQL-like search language.
  • Arkime (formerly Moloch) — full-packet indexing for retrospective hunting; web UI.
  • NetworkMiner — extracts files, certificates, and credentials from a pcap. Different mental model from Wireshark — useful contrast.

1. The scenario you are walking into

You are a tier-1 SOC (Security Operations Center) analyst at Plateau Realty Inc., a fictitious mid-size Montréal property-management firm with ~250 employees, four downtown branches, and one IT manager. Yesterday at 22:13 EST, Plateau Realty's liaison to the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (CCCS) received a short note from CCCS's monitoring partner programme:

"One of your public IP addresses appeared in our threat-feed last night as a likely outbound beacon. The destination has been associated with a known crime-ware family. We recommend you look at the timeframe 02:00 – 02:30 EST on host(s) that egressed through 198.51.100.42."

It is now 09:00 EST the next morning. Your shift lead has dropped three evidence files into your lab share and assigned you the triage:

File Source Size
plateau_22h00_22h30.pcapng SPAN mirror, Marketing VLAN (Virtual Local Area Network) ~120 MB
suspicious_files/ (8 files) EDR-quarantined items from one workstation varies
dc01_security.evtx Windows Security log of the domain controller ~14 MB

Your deliverable, by end of shift, is a methodical triage of all three lanes, a correlation across them, and a clear next-action recommendation.

This is exactly the discipline taught in Lecture 2: the three lanes — Traffic, Files, Event Logs — and lane agreement as the measure of confidence.

  3. Record the verdict.

5.2 Hunt 4625 bursts

Event ID 4625 is "An account failed to log on". A burst of 4625s from the same source IP — especially against the same target account — is the textbook brute-force / password-spray signature.

Run, in PowerShell:

cd C:\cyber-soho-code\
.\L2_hunt_4625.ps1 -Path '\\share\cohort-cyb01\lab4\dc01_security.evtx' -BucketMinutes 1

The script outputs a per-minute bucket of 4625 counts. Screenshot the bucket where the count spikes.

Screenshot

📸 Screenshot 11. Burst bucket from L2_hunt_4625.ps1 — the minute where 4625 count > 10. Caption: "4625 burst at 22:07 EST — 32 failed logons in one minute from source 198.51.100.42."

5.3 Did anything succeed?

In Event Viewer:

  • Filter Current Log → Event ID 4624 (logon success).
  • Filter the XML view for LogonType = 10 (Remote Desktop Protocol — RDP) or LogonType = 3 (Network).
  • Look for a 4624 from the same source IP as the 4625 burst, within ~2 minutes of the burst tailing off.

A 4625 burst followed by exactly one 4624 from the same source is the spray-then-success signature.

6. Step 4 — Correlate the three lanes (30 minutes)

This is the step that turns triage into evidence.

Build a chronological correlation table:

Time (EST) Lane 1 (traffic) Lane 2 (files) Lane 3 (logs) Inference

Fill rows for every minute that has an event in any lane. Empty cells are not failures — they are questions: an empty cell tells you which lane you should still go look at.

Then write a half-page conclusion (no more than 250 words) answering exactly three questions:

6. Step 4 — Apply forensic analysis techniques (home — 1.5 hours)

Pick two of the four phases for a deeper dive at home. The deeper dive is what separates a junior triage report from an evidence-grade analysis.

6.1 For the credential-dumping phase (Phase 2)

Open Sysmon-Operational in Event Viewer. Filter to Event ID 10 (Process Access). Look at events where:

  • TargetImage = C:\Windows\system32\lsass.exe
  • GrantedAccess value is suspicious. Common high-signal masks:
  • 0x1010 — PROCESS_QUERY_LIMITED_INFORMATION + PROCESS_VM_READ. Mimikatz-style.
  • 0x1410 — even more capability, including PROCESS_VM_OPERATION.
  • 0x1FFFFF — full access. Almost always malicious unless the source is lsass.exe itself.
  • SourceImage is not a known SYSTEM tool (i.e., not wininit.exe, not services.exe).

Document:

  • The exact GrantedAccess value seen.
  • The SourceImage and its full path.
  • The CallTrace if Sysmon recorded it.

Lab Safety — What to Do, and What NOT to DoSafety

›

The labs in this course are run inside a deliberately isolated, instructor-controlled environment. The discipline of treating that environment with the same rigour you would treat a production network is part of what is being taught. Some rules are inviolable.

What you MUST do

  1. Confirm authorisation in writing. Every action you take in this lab is authorised by the instructor for the duration of the cohort. Lack of authorisation, even for a "harmless" test, is a criminal offence in Canada under Criminal Code section 342.1 and parallel statutes in every G20 jurisdiction (see the closing disclaimer).
  2. Use only the provided evidence. Do not bring external malware samples into the lab share. Do not download samples from the internet onto a personal device.
  3. Look up hashes, not files. When using VirusTotal, search by SHA-256 — never upload the file. Uploading publishes the sample to a public reputation service; if it contains PII (Personally Identifiable Information), credentials, or proprietary data, you have leaked it.
  4. Time-stamp every observation. Chain of custody requires it.
  5. Hash every evidence file before touching it. sha256sum is your friend.
  6. Run scripts in your own user account, not as root, unless a step explicitly requires it.
  7. Snapshot your VM before you begin a destructive step (e.g. running a triage script that writes output).
  8. Verify every external URL through VirusTotal URL Scanner before clicking — a 15-second habit that has saved at least one student per cohort.

What you MUST NOT do

  1. Do not replicate any attack technique outside the lab against any system you do not personally own and have written permission to test. The line between training and crime is authorisation, and authorisation must be in writing.
  2. Do not exfiltrate any lab artefact off campus. The pcap, the .evtx, the suspicious files — they stay in the lab share.
  3. Do not paste personal credentials into any helper script. The scripts are for demonstration, and they log to local files.
  4. Do not run the credential-dump phase of Lab 5 on any personal machine. Mimikatz and equivalents are flagged by your antivirus for a reason.
  5. Do not click external links from inside a pcap-extracted artefact. Use VirusTotal-by-hash or VT-URL-search; never let the artefact itself dictate where your browser goes.
  6. Do not store evidence on shared cloud drives (OneDrive, Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud). Use the lab share or an encrypted local volume.
  7. Do not submit by email. OmniVox LEA Dropbox only. Email is non-confidential and non-tracked.
  8. Do not share your written report with classmates before grading — plagiarism is grounds for a zero per the JAC IPESA Policy No. 7 and Policy No. 13 Code of Conduct; the same penalties apply to giving as to receiving.
